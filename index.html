How drunk, when

0.02%Slightly relaxed. French novice-driver limit.
0.05%Clear buzz, chattier, judgement starting to slip. French driving limit.
0.08%Properly drunk: coordination and reaction time noticeably off.
0.10–0.15%Slurring, clumsy, memory gaps start.
0.20%+Blackout territory, nausea, real risk.

BAC is blood alcohol concentration: grams of ethanol per 100 ml of blood, shown as a percentage. 0.05% is the same as 0.5 g/L or 0.5‰. The body clears a fixed ~0.015% per hour — about 8 g of ethanol an hour for an 80 kg man, closer to 5 g for a smaller body — so for most people even one 10 g drink an hour slowly stacks up. Food, sleep, and body weight all move the curve; coffee and water don't sober you up, they just pass the time.

In "same alcohol" mode the curve is identical for vodka and wine — 10 g of ethanol is 10 g. Switch to "same number of glasses" to see a shot per hour vs a glass per hour. Simple Widmark estimate: each drink lands instantly, liver clears ~0.015% per hour (≈8 g for an 80 kg man). Real absorption is slower on a full stomach and faster on an empty one or with diet mixers (peak up to ~18% higher).

A unit = 10 g ethanol (Belgian / Dutch standard). "kcal / unit" is the number that matters: it's what a given buzz costs you, regardless of glass size.

Still wines use dry / off-dry / medium / sweet (or trocken / halbtrocken / lieblich / süß). If the label says nothing, the alcohol % is the tell: a sparkling at 5–7% stopped fermenting early and kept its sugar — assume 50–140 g/L. Since 2023, EU wine labels must list kcal per 100 ml, often behind a QR code; that's the exact number to use.
